## SAMPLE 1: Lord of the Flies - Simon Analysis

### Task Prompt
Analyze the character of Simon in William Golding's Lord of the Flies. Examine how Golding uses this character to develop the novel's themes. Support your analysis with specific evidence from the text.

### Student Response
Simon is different from all the other boys on the island and I think Golding made him that way on purpose to show something about human nature that the other characters can't show. He's the only one who understands what the beast really is. When he says "maybe it's only us" he's basically figured out the whole point of the novel before anyone else. That's really significant.

The place where Simon goes to be alone is described like a church almost. Golding writes about the candle buds and how the light comes through and it's peaceful. This is different from everywhere else on the island which is chaotic especially as the story goes on. I think Simon needs this place because he can see things the others can't and it's overwhelming. He has seizures too which in ancient times people thought meant you could talk to gods or see visions. Golding probably knew this.

When Simon confronts the Lord of the Flies it's like a psychological battle. The pig head talks to him and says "I'm part of you" which connects back to what Simon said earlier about the beast being inside them. This is the climax of the theme really. The beast isn't a physical thing it's the capacity for evil that exists in everyone. Simon understands this but the other boys are too scared to accept it. They need the beast to be external so they can fight it or hide from it.

His death is written like a sacrifice. The language Golding uses is really elevated and beautiful compared to the rest of the book. "The great wave of the tide moved farther along the island and the water lifted" and his body is surrounded by glowing creatures. This is like a religious painting almost. Meanwhile the other boys have become complete savages doing their hunting dance. The contrast is intentional.

What makes this really tragic is that Simon was going to tell them the truth. He found out the beast was just a dead parachutist—a man who fell from the sky—and he was coming down the mountain to explain. But they killed the messenger. In Christianity the prophets are often killed by the people they're trying to save. Golding is saying that humanity rejects the truth when it's uncomfortable.

Ralph crying at the end "for the darkness of man's heart" shows he finally understood what Simon knew all along. But it took everything falling apart for him to see it. Simon saw it from the beginning because he was different. Whether that makes him a saint or just an outsider who could see more clearly because he wasn't caught up in the power struggles, that's an interesting question.

The other boys wanted to be rescued from the island but Simon knew they needed to be rescued from themselves.

## SAMPLE 2: The Great Gatsby - American Dream

### Task Prompt
Analyze how F. Scott Fitzgerald presents the American Dream in The Great Gatsby. Examine the author's techniques and their effects on the reader. Support your analysis with specific evidence from the text.

### Student Response
In F. Scott Fitzgerald's seminal novel The Great Gatsby, the author presents a nuanced and multifaceted exploration of the American Dream and its profound implications for the characters who pursue it. Through sophisticated literary techniques, Fitzgerald crafts a devastating critique of 1920s society that continues to resonate with contemporary readers. This essay will examine three key aspects of Fitzgerald's presentation: the symbolic green light, the contrast between East and West Egg, and the characterization of Jay Gatsby himself.

The green light at the end of Daisy's dock serves as the novel's most prominent symbol of the American Dream. Fitzgerald positions this image at crucial moments throughout the narrative, establishing its significance from Nick's first observation of Gatsby "stretching out his arms toward the dark water." The green light represents Gatsby's hopes and dreams, connecting his personal desires to the broader American ethos of aspiration. By the novel's conclusion, the symbol has expanded to encompass all of humanity's striving for a better future.

Furthermore, Fitzgerald employs the geographical contrast between East Egg and West Egg to illustrate the social stratification of American society. The established wealth of East Egg, where the Buchanans reside, stands in stark opposition to the nouveau riche world of West Egg, where Gatsby has constructed his magnificent mansion. This spatial arrangement reflects the deeper divisions within the American class system, suggesting that the Dream's promise of social mobility may be more illusory than real. The author's careful attention to setting thus reinforces the thematic concerns of the novel.

The characterization of Gatsby himself provides the most compelling vehicle for Fitzgerald's exploration of the American Dream. Gatsby's transformation from James Gatz to Jay Gatsby embodies the self-invention that lies at the heart of the American mythos. His accumulation of wealth, his lavish parties, and his single-minded pursuit of Daisy all demonstrate the lengths to which individuals will go in pursuit of their dreams. Yet Fitzgerald also reveals the hollowness at the core of Gatsby's achievement, suggesting that material success cannot ultimately satisfy the deeper yearnings of the human heart.

In conclusion, The Great Gatsby remains a masterful examination of the American Dream and its discontents. Through his sophisticated use of symbolism, setting, and characterization, Fitzgerald creates a timeless critique of the pursuit of wealth and status. The tragic fate of Gatsby serves as a cautionary tale, reminding readers that the green light of aspiration, however beautiful, may lead only to disillusionment. Fitzgerald's novel thus stands as an enduring testament to the complexities of the American experience and the eternal human struggle between dreams and reality.

## SAMPLE 3: Persuasive Speech - Social Media Age Restrictions

### Task Prompt
Write a persuasive speech arguing for or against raising the minimum age for social media use from 13 to 16. Your speech should be suitable for delivery to your school's parent-teacher association.

### Student Response
Good evening parents and teachers. Today I want to talk to you about an important issue that affects all of our young people: social media. I believe that the minimum age for social media should be raised from 13 to 16, and I will explain my reasons why.

First of all, social media can have negative effects on mental health. Studies have shown that teenagers who spend a lot of time on social media are more likely to experience anxiety and depression. Young people compare themselves to others online and this can make them feel bad about themselves. The images they see are often edited and unrealistic, but teenagers don't always understand this. By raising the age to 16, we can protect younger adolescents during a vulnerable time in their development.

Second, social media exposes children to inappropriate content. Even with parental controls and age restrictions, young people can still encounter things they shouldn't see. This includes cyberbullying, which is a serious problem in our schools. When children are older, they are better equipped to handle these situations and make good decisions about what to engage with online.

Third, social media can be a distraction from schoolwork. Many students spend hours scrolling through their feeds when they should be doing homework or sleeping. This affects their grades and their health. At 16, students are more mature and can better balance their time between social media and their responsibilities.

Some people might argue that social media helps teenagers stay connected with friends and learn digital skills they will need in the future. I understand this concern. However, there are other ways for young people to stay in touch with friends and learn technology. The risks of early social media use outweigh the benefits.

In conclusion, I strongly believe that raising the social media age to 16 would benefit our children. It would protect their mental health, shield them from inappropriate content, and help them focus on their education. I urge all parents and teachers here tonight to support this change. Our children's wellbeing should be our top priority. Thank you for listening.

## SAMPLE 4: Ozymandias Poetry Analysis

### Task Prompt
Analyze how Percy Bysshe Shelley conveys ideas about power and time in "Ozymandias." Examine the poet's use of literary techniques and their effects. Support your analysis with specific evidence from the text.

### Student Response
The poem Ozymandias is about how power doesnt last forever and even the most powerful kings become nothing. Shelley uses the statue that is broken in the desert to show this idea. But what is really clever is HOW he shows it, not just that he shows it.

The structure of the poem is like russian dolls. Shelley is telling us what a traveller told him about what the sculptor made of what the king said. So we are four levels away from Ozymandias himself. This is already making the king distant and small. His words "look on my works ye mighty and despair" come to us through so many filters that they lose there power. The king wanted to speak directly to other kings to scare them but now his message is like a whisper passed through many mouths. Shelley is showing that even communication of power decays over time not just physical monuments.

The irony is very complex. Ozymandias wanted people to despair because his works were so great but now we despair because everything is gone. The meaning completely fliped. "Nothing beside remains" makes the boasting become sad instead of scary. But theres another level too - the sculptor who "well those passions read" actually outlasted the king in a way. The art survived when the power didn't. So maybe Shelley is also saying something about artists versus kings, whose work actually matters more in the long run.

I think the desert is important too. "Boundless and bare the lone and level sands stretch far away" - all those L sounds and B sounds make it feel empty and endless. The desert doesn't care about human achievement. Time is like the desert, it just erases everything equally whether your a slave or a king. Shelley puts the human drama (the statue, the boasting) against this huge indifferent background and shows how small it all is.

The "sneer of cold command" is still visible on the statue face. So the personality of the tyrant survived through the sculptors skill. The king is remember but not how he wanted to be remember. He wanted fear and respect but instead we see his arrogance and feel sorry for his foolishness. The sculptor captured truth about power that the powerful person couldnt see about himself. This is maybe the deepest irony - the artist understood the king better then the king understood himself.

Shelley is a romantic poet and they believed in nature over civilization, art over politics. This poem is proof of that philosophy.
