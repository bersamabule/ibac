## What Criterion D Assesses

Criterion D evaluates a student's capacity for **linguistic competence**—the ability to use language as a tool for communication and effect. It measures the journey from language as obstacle to language as invisible art.

---

## The Five Strands

### Strand (i): Vocabulary and Sentence Structures
> "use appropriate and varied vocabulary, sentence structures and forms of expression"

**What this means:** Word choice should be precise and varied; sentence structures should demonstrate range.

### Strand (ii): Register and Style
> "write and speak in a register and style that serve the context and intention"

**What this means:** The formality, tone, and voice should be appropriate to the genre and purpose.

### Strand (iii): Grammar and Syntax
> "use correct grammar, syntax and punctuation"

**What this means:** Mechanical accuracy supports communication.

### Strand (iv): Spelling and Accuracy
> "spell (alphabetic languages), write (character languages) and pronounce with accuracy"

**What this means:** Mechanical precision is expected.

### Strand (v): Non-verbal Communication
> "use appropriate non-verbal communication techniques"

**What this means:** For spoken/presented work, body language and visual elements matter.

## The Linguistic Trajectory

Criterion D tracks the development of language mastery:

| Level | Archetype | Relationship to Language |
|-------|-----------|-------------------------|
| 1-2 | The Phonetic Transcriber | Language as obstacle |
| 3-4 | The IKEA Builder | Language as functional tool |
| 5-6 | The Custom Carpenter | Language as shaped material |
| 7-8 | The Glassblower | Language as embodied art |

---

## Level 1-2: The Phonetic Transcriber

### Core Metaphor
A person transcribing speech, writing what they hear in their head. The gap between spoken and written English creates errors, and language is a barrier to communication rather than a tool for it.

### Relationship to Language
**Language as obstacle.** The student struggles with the code of written English. They transcribe oral speech rather than construct written text.

### Characteristic Behaviors
- Homophone errors ("could of," "then/than," "there/their")
- Run-on sentences (comma splices, fused sentences)
- Limited vocabulary—reliance on Tier 1 (basic) words
- Intensifiers carry meaning ("really," "very," "so")
- Register confusion (conversational in formal contexts)

### Key Diagnostic Frameworks

**The Phonetic Transcriber Concept:**
Level 1-2 students write what they hear in their heads. Errors like "could of" (for "could have") reveal that they're transcribing speech patterns, not constructing written language.

**The Tier 1 Trap:**
Level 1-2 students rely on basic conversational vocabulary and use intensifiers ("really," "very") to carry meaning that precise vocabulary should convey:
- "The book was really really sad" vs. "The book was devastating"

**The Error Threshold:**
Level 1-2 errors "often hinder" communication—the reader must decode the meaning.

### Diagnostic Markers
- **Homophone Test:** Are there sound-based spelling errors?
- **Sentence Boundary Test:** Are sentences properly demarcated?
- **Vocabulary Range:** Tier 1 dominance with intensifier reliance?
- **Register Match:** Does formality match the genre?

---

## Level 3-4: The IKEA Builder

### Core Metaphor
A person assembling IKEA furniture. They follow the instructions correctly, the product is functional, but there's no personal craft—just competent assembly of prefabricated parts.

### Relationship to Language
**Language as functional tool.** The student can construct correct, serviceable prose. Writing is functional but formulaic—an academic "uniform" rather than a distinctive voice.

### Characteristic Behaviors
- Functional vocabulary (appropriate but not varied)
- Connected sentences using conjunctions
- Genre-compliant register
- Mostly accurate mechanics ("speed bumps" rather than barriers)
- Borrowed imagery and clichés
- Monotonous rhythm

### The "Template Quality"
Level 3-4 writing is correct but formulaic. The student wears an academic "uniform"—the writing is appropriate but interchangeable. Any competent student could have produced it.

### The Error Threshold
Level 3-4 errors "sometimes hinder"—they are speed bumps, not roadblocks. The reader notices errors but can still follow the meaning.

### Diagnostic Markers
- **The Uniform Test:** Could any student have written this?
- **The Rhythm Test:** Is there sentence variety?
- **The Cliché Count:** How many borrowed phrases?
- **The Voice Test:** Is there authentic voice or just correctness?

---

## Level 5-6: The Custom Carpenter

### Core Metaphor
A skilled carpenter who crafts from scratch rather than assembling prefabricated parts. The work is solid, functional, and aesthetically pleasing—you can see the skill, though you can also see the effort. The sanding marks are still visible.

### Relationship to Language
**Language as shaped material.** The student consciously manipulates language for effect. Vocabulary is precise, syntax is purposeful, register is secure. But the effort is still visible—this is skilled craft, not yet virtuosic art.

### Characteristic Behaviors
- Precise vocabulary (words chosen for exact meaning)
- Purposeful syntax variety
- Secure register
- Invisible mechanics (errors do not hinder)
- Authentic voice (distinctive, not generic)
- Visible effort—you can see the skill at work

### The Error Threshold
Level 5-6 errors "do not hinder"—mechanics are essentially invisible. The reader doesn't notice the machinery because it works smoothly.

### Diagnostic Markers
- **The Precision Test:** Are words chosen for exact effect?
- **The Syntax Variety Test:** Is there purposeful variation?
- **The Invisibility Test:** Do mechanics disappear?
- **The Effort Visibility:** Can you see the skill at work?

---

## Level 7-8: The Glassblower

### Core Metaphor
An artisan glassblower who transforms molten material into something that appears to have no substance—pure light and form. The craft is so complete that the medium becomes transparent. You don't see glass; you see through it.

### Relationship to Language
**Language as invisible art.** Language is no longer a tool being used—it is embodied thought. The syntax IS the argument. The vocabulary illuminates rather than just describes. The reader forgets they are reading words.

### Characteristic Behaviors
- Illuminating vocabulary (words that reframe, not just describe)
- Embodied syntax (form mirrors content)
- Effortless register (no seams between modes)
- Flawless polish (professional standard)
- Language as transparent medium
- Craft invisible because it's complete

### The Key Distinction: Competence vs. Artistry
The shift from Level 5-6 to Level 7-8 is not about fewer errors or better vocabulary—it's about language becoming transparent. The reader stops noticing the language and experiences pure meaning.

### Diagnostic Markers
- **The Transparency Test:** Does language disappear?
- **The Illumination Test:** Do words reveal new understanding?
- **The Embodiment Test:** Does form mirror content?
- **The Professional Test:** Could this be published?

## The Compensation Rule: Linguistic Floor (D Supports A)

Weak language does NOT automatically lower analysis scores, provided meaning remains accessible.

**Key Question:** Can the reader access the student's thinking despite the language errors?

- If YES → Assess A based on the thinking, not the expression
- If NO → The inaccessibility may limit A

**Why this matters:** This rule protects the "Thinker in Translation"—students whose intellectual sophistication exceeds their linguistic development. Their ideas deserve fair assessment.

## Vocabulary Tier Framework

| Tier | Description | Examples | Level Association |
|------|-------------|----------|-------------------|
| Tier 1 | Basic, high-frequency words | good, bad, said, happy | Level 1-2 (dominant) |
| Tier 2 | Academic, cross-domain words | analyze, contrast, significant | Level 3-4+ |
| Tier 3 | Domain-specific technical terms | metaphor, enjambment, rhetoric | Level 5-6+ |

**Diagnostic Use:** Tier 1 dominance with intensifier reliance suggests Level 1-2. Appropriate Tier 2/3 vocabulary suggests Level 3-4+.

## Intervention Strategies

| Level | Strategy | Implementation |
|-------|----------|----------------|
| 1-2 → 3-4 | "The Toolbox" | Pattern-based error correction—target 2-3 high-frequency errors |
| 3-4 → 5-6 | "The Word Choice Audit" | Replace generic words with precise alternatives |
| 5-6 → 7-8 | "The Syntax Laboratory" | Experiment with form mirroring content |
