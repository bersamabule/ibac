# Cross-Criteria Student Archetypes

**Module:** `05_CROSS_CRITERIA/CC1_student_archetypes.md`  
**Version:** 1.0  
**Last Updated:** 2025-12-05

---

## Purpose

Real student work rarely shows uniform performance across all criteria. These six archetypes capture common patterns of uneven performance, enabling:
- Rapid diagnostic classification
- Targeted feedback design
- Intervention prioritization
- Resistance to the Halo Effect

---

## The Six Archetypes

### 1. The Unbound Architect

**Profile:** High A, Low B

**Pattern:** Has great ideas but no organizational blueprint. The thinking is sophisticated, but the presentation is chaotic.

**Characteristic Work:**
- Brilliant insights scattered throughout
- Reader must work to follow the argument
- Paragraphs could be reordered without obvious loss
- Transitions missing or illogical
- Ideas connect in the writer's mind but not on the page

**Diagnostic Markers:**
- Strong analysis buried in poor structure
- Reader says "I think I see what you mean, but..."
- Individual sentences are excellent; overall coherence is weak

**Limiting Factor:** Criterion B (Organization)

**Feedback Approach:**
> "Brilliant bricks, unstable wall. Your ideas are too good to be lost in the chaos. Next challenge: Outline before you write."

**Intervention Strategy:** "The Skeleton"—reverse outlining to expose structure gaps. Have student outline their work AFTER writing to see the architecture (or lack thereof).

---

### 2. The Polished Hollow

**Profile:** High B/C/D, Low A

**Pattern:** Beautiful facade with nothing inside. Mastery of form paired with absence of thought. The writing sounds impressive but says little.

**Characteristic Work:**
- Elegant sentences, sophisticated vocabulary
- Well-organized, clear structure
- Follows conventions perfectly
- Analysis is surface-level or circular
- Style masks absence of substance

**Diagnostic Markers:**
- Reader is impressed initially, then asks "But what are they actually saying?"
- Technical execution exceeds cognitive engagement
- "Sounds smart" but doesn't withstand scrutiny

**Limiting Factor:** Criterion A (Analysis/Thinking)

**Feedback Approach:**
> "You are playing it too safe. Your writing is beautiful, but beauty without meaning is decoration. Be wrong, but be interesting."

**Intervention Strategy:** "The Why Game"—before accepting any claim, demand explanation of mechanism. Push past the first answer.

**Compensation Rule Triggered:** Style ≠ Substance. No amount of linguistic polish compensates for analytical absence.

---

### 3. The Thinker in Translation

**Profile:** High A, Low D

**Pattern:** Intellectually mature, linguistically emerging. The ideas are sophisticated but trapped in limited language. Language is the only barrier.

**Characteristic Work:**
- Complex ideas, simple vocabulary
- Grammatical errors that don't obscure meaning
- Frustrating gap between thought and expression
- Reader can see the intelligence struggling against the medium
- Often an ELL (English Language Learner) or developing writer

**Diagnostic Markers:**
- Strong analysis despite weak mechanics
- Reader says "I see what they're trying to say"
- Ideas exceed vocabulary range

**Limiting Factor:** Criterion D (Language)

**Feedback Approach:**
> "Your ideas are too good to be hidden by grammar mistakes. The thinking is already sophisticated—now we build the language to match."

**Intervention Strategy:** "The Toolbox"—pattern-based error correction focused on highest-frequency mistakes. Don't overwhelm; target 2-3 patterns at a time.

**Compensation Rule Triggered:** Linguistic Floor. Weak language does NOT automatically lower analysis scores if meaning remains accessible.

---

### 4. The Checkbox Champion

**Profile:** Moderate across all criteria

**Pattern:** Does exactly what is asked, nothing more. Compliance over curiosity. Safe, competent, forgettable.

**Characteristic Work:**
- Meets all requirements
- No risks taken
- Formulaic approach
- Correct but uninspired
- Could be written by any student following instructions

**Diagnostic Markers:**
- Reader has no complaints but no praise
- "Perfectly adequate" is highest compliment
- Nothing memorable or distinctive

**Limiting Factor:** Not a single criterion—a disposition toward safety

**Feedback Approach:**
> "Competence achieved. You've proven you can follow instructions. Next challenge: Subversion. What happens if you break a rule on purpose?"

**Intervention Strategy:** "The Sandbox"—low-stakes creative exercises where risk is rewarded. Cap "safe" work at Level 5; require risk for higher levels.

---

### 5. The Risk-Averse Technician

**Profile:** High B/D, Moderate A, Low C

**Pattern:** High skill, low courage. Would rather be correct than interesting. Technical mastery without creative engagement.

**Characteristic Work:**
- Flawless mechanics
- Clear organization
- Solid but safe analysis
- No creative risks
- Avoids anything that might be "wrong"

**Diagnostic Markers:**
- Reader respects the craft but feels nothing
- Work is admirable but not moving
- Student could do more but chooses not to

**Limiting Factor:** Criterion C (Creativity/Text Production)

**Feedback Approach:**
> "Your technical skills are excellent—now use them to take risks. What would you write if you couldn't be wrong?"

**Intervention Strategy:** "The Risky Draft Policy"—explicitly reward formal and interpretive risk-taking. Make safety a ceiling, not a floor.

---

### 6. The Intuitive Rambler

**Profile:** High C, Moderate A, Low B

**Pattern:** Writes from gut/heart with great energy but gets lost. Strong creative instincts, weak structural discipline.

**Characteristic Work:**
- Passionate, energetic voice
- Genuine creative engagement
- Ideas emerge through writing (not before)
- Reader enjoys moments but loses the thread
- No clear destination; writing discovers its own meaning

**Diagnostic Markers:**
- Reader says "I love parts of this, but where is it going?"
- Strong voice, weak architecture
- Creative energy exceeds organizational control

**Limiting Factor:** Criterion B (Organization)

**Feedback Approach:**
> "Your voice is authentic and your energy is infectious. The challenge: Can you channel that energy into a structure that serves your meaning?"

**Intervention Strategy:** "The GPS"—after free-writing, identify the destination. Then rebuild the path to get there.

## Using Archetypes in Assessment

### Step 1: Profile the Student
After assessing all relevant criteria, identify which archetype best fits the pattern:
- Which criterion is strongest?
- Which criterion is weakest (the limiting factor)?
- Does the pattern match a known archetype?

### Step 2: Identify the Limiting Factor
The limiting factor is the criterion that most constrains overall performance. Focus intervention here.

### Step 3: Design Targeted Feedback
Use archetype-appropriate feedback that:
- Validates genuine strengths
- Names the limiting factor honestly
- Provides actionable next step
- Motivates rather than demoralizes

### Step 4: Apply Compensation Rules
Check whether compensation rules affect the assessment:
- Does structural chaos cap analysis scores? (Structural Ceiling)
- Does linguistic weakness obscure meaning? (Linguistic Floor)
- Is style masking substance gaps? (Style ≠ Substance)

## The Priority Matrix

| If Limiting Factor Is... | Instructional Priority | Strategy |
|--------------------------|------------------------|----------|
| Criterion A (Thinking) | Epistemology & Inquiry | "The Why Game"—thinking routines before writing |
| Criterion B (Organization) | Architecture & Logic | "The Skeleton"—reverse outlining to expose structure |
| Criterion C (Creativity) | Aesthetics & Risk | "The Sandbox"—low-stakes creative exercises |
| Criterion D (Language) | Mechanics & Clarity | "The Toolbox"—pattern-based error correction |
