# Compensation Rules

**Module:** `05_CROSS_CRITERIA/CC2_compensation_rules.md`  
**Version:** 1.0  
**Last Updated:** 2025-12-05

---

## Purpose

Compensation rules describe how performance in one criterion affects the assessment of others. They prevent both the Halo Effect (strength inflating other scores) and unfair penalization (weakness in one area inappropriately lowering another).

These rules are **SOURCE CODE** for the Expert System and must be applied rigidly.

---

## Rule 1: The Structural Ceiling (B Limits A)

### Statement
Chaotic organization caps how high analysis can be rated. If insights cannot be followed, they cannot be assessed as sustained or coherent.

### Mechanism
Criterion A requires demonstrating "sustained" and "coherent" analysis. But if the organizational structure (Criterion B) is chaotic, the reader cannot verify that the analysis is sustained—even if brilliant insights exist scattered throughout the work.

### Application

| Criterion B Level | Maximum Criterion A Level |
|-------------------|---------------------------|
| Level 1-2 | Capped at Level 4 |
| Level 3-4 | Capped at Level 5-6 |
| Level 5-6 | No ceiling |
| Level 7-8 | No ceiling |

### Example
A student produces scattered but brilliant analytical observations. The reader can identify moments of Level 6 thinking, but the disorganization makes it impossible to verify that the analysis is "sustained." The work cannot receive above Level 4 for Criterion A because the structural chaos prevents assessment of coherence.

### Exception
If individual paragraphs are internally coherent and contain verifiable sustained analysis, partial credit may be given—but the overall ceiling still applies.

## Rule 2: The Linguistic Floor (D Supports A)

### Statement
Weak language does NOT automatically lower analysis scores, provided meaning remains accessible.

### Mechanism
Criterion D assesses language use; Criterion A assesses analytical thinking. A student with limited English proficiency (or developing writing skills) may produce grammatically imperfect work that still demonstrates sophisticated thinking. The language weakness should be assessed under Criterion D, not used to penalize Criterion A.

### Application

| Criterion D Level | Effect on Criterion A |
|-------------------|----------------------|
| Level 1-2, meaning obscured | May lower A (reader cannot access thinking) |
| Level 1-2, meaning accessible | No penalty to A |
| Level 3-4 or higher | No penalty to A |

### Key Question
**Can the reader access the student's thinking despite the language errors?**

- If YES → Assess A based on the thinking, not the expression
- If NO → The inaccessibility may limit A (but note this in feedback)

### Example
A student writes: "The author make the reader feel alone because metaphor of island show isolation even when people around." The grammar is weak (Level 2-3), but the analytical insight is clear: the island metaphor creates isolation amid presence. Criterion A should reflect the analytical thinking; Criterion D should reflect the language limitations.

### The "Thinker in Translation"
This rule protects the "Thinker in Translation" archetype—students whose intellectual sophistication exceeds their linguistic development. Their ideas should be assessed fairly.

## Rule 3: Style ≠ Substance (D/C ≠ A)

### Statement
No amount of beautiful vocabulary or creative voice compensates for absence of analytical thinking. The container is not the contents.

### Mechanism
Students (and teachers) can be seduced by elegant prose into overestimating analytical depth. A beautifully written essay that says nothing substantive is still empty. Style is assessed under Criteria C and D; substance is assessed under Criterion A.

### Application
When assessing Criterion A, ask:
1. Strip away the style. What is the student actually arguing?
2. Is there genuine analytical insight, or just elegant assertion?
3. Could this analysis be expressed in plain language and still be substantive?

### The "Polished Hollow" Test
If the work sounds impressive but you cannot paraphrase the actual argument, it may be a "Polished Hollow"—high C/D, low A.

### Example
A student writes: "Shakespeare's intricate tapestry of linguistic virtuosity weaves a profound meditation on the ephemeral nature of human ambition, crafting through his masterful deployment of imagery a resonant commentary that echoes through the corridors of time."

This sentence is stylistically sophisticated but analytically empty. It makes no specific claim, identifies no specific technique, and explains no specific effect. Criterion D might be Level 5-6 (varied vocabulary, complex syntax); Criterion A would be Level 1-2 (no actual analysis).

### Diagnostic Question
**"What is the student actually saying beneath the style?"**

If the answer is "nothing specific," style is masking substance gaps.

## Applying Compensation Rules in Assessment

### Step 1: Assess Each Criterion Independently (First Pass)
Before applying compensation rules, assess each criterion on its own merits. What level does the work achieve for A? For B? For C? For D?

### Step 2: Check for Compensation Effects (Second Pass)
After initial assessment, check:
- Does low B create a ceiling for A?
- Does low D obscure meaning (affecting A)?
- Is high C/D masking low A?

### Step 3: Adjust if Necessary
If compensation rules apply, adjust the affected criterion AND note the compensation in your assessment rationale.

### Step 4: Provide Targeted Feedback
Use compensation patterns to guide feedback:
- If Structural Ceiling is active → prioritize organization
- If Linguistic Floor is active → acknowledge thinking while building language
- If Style ≠ Substance is active → push for analytical depth

## Preventing the Halo Effect

The Halo Effect occurs when strength in one criterion inappropriately inflates scores in another. Compensation rules work in both directions:

**Positive Halo (Prevent):**
- Beautiful writing (high D) should not inflate analysis scores (A)
- Creative voice (high C) should not inflate organization scores (B)
- Clear structure (high B) should not inflate analytical depth (A)

**Negative Halo (Prevent):**
- Weak grammar (low D) should not deflate analysis scores (A) if meaning is clear
- Unconventional structure (low B) should not deflate creativity scores (C) if the choice is intentional

### The Two-Pass Method
The Two-Pass Grading Method (see `06_ASSESSMENT_PROTOCOLS/AP1_two_pass_grading.md`) is specifically designed to prevent Halo Effects by separating container assessment from contents assessment.
